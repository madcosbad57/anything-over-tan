Salsa over Tan: a Jaguar F-Type at the Circuit de Gueux

A summer in a V6 convertible, a Belgian flag, and the slow, dawning realisation that the cheapest proper British sports car of the last twenty years is currently sitting in classified ads under £20,000.

ByMarco Vieth

The Belgian flag was, frankly, the giveaway. We were three days into a slow, lopsided loop through northern France — Calais to Reims, Reims to the Ardennes, the Ardennes to a hotel in Spa with a shower that didn't work — when we pulled the F-Type into the old pit lane at Gueux and found ourselves staring up at a tricolour we had not, strictly speaking, expected.

The Circuit de Gueux is not, in the technical sense, a circuit any more. It hosted the French Grand Prix six times between 1951 and 1966, then quietly stopped, and most of the road it ran on became, again, a public road. What is left is the start-finish straight, the pit garages, the timekeepers' tower, and the grandstand — preserved, lovingly, by a group of volunteers who call themselves Les amis du circuit de Gueux. They paint the Esso and Castrol signs every spring. They mow the grass. They are, on the day we visited, also responsible for the flagpole, which was flying Belgian for reasons no one we asked could quite explain.

This is the kind of place you stumble into and never quite leave. We had three hours and a half-charged camera battery. We left with most of a roll of Velvia and a stronger conviction than we'd arrived with: that the F-Type V6 convertible, currently sitting in classified ads from £20,000, is the most undervalued proper British sports car on sale today. Possibly the last one.

What it is, and what it isn't

The F-Type was always more grand tourer than track weapon, and the V6 cars — both the 335-hp base car and the 375-hp V6 S — wear that mission better than the bigger-engined R variants ever did. The supercharged 3.0 makes the right noises (Jaguar fitted active exhaust valves on the S; tap the chequered-flag button and you can wake people up two streets over), the chassis is set up for a long sweeping bend rather than a hairpin, and the steering, while electric, is the better-judged of the two systems Jaguar shipped in this era.

It is also, importantly, the right shape. Ian Callum's last great Jaguar before he left the company is, depending on the angle, an E-Type that grew up, an XK that lost weight, or — at Gueux, in profile, against a row of mid-century pit garages — a car that looks like it was always meant to be parked there. The proportions are correct. The roof is canvas, not folding tin. And in Salsa Red over Tan, in summer light, against a whitewashed concrete wall painted with sixty-year-old advertisements for engine oil, it does the thing that very few modern cars manage to do: it looks like an event.

"It looks like it was always meant to be parked there."— Notebook, Gueux pit lane, 11:42

What the F-Type isn't, and we should be honest about this, is a Porsche. The eight-speed ZF auto is a more relaxed gearbox than a PDK; the V6 cars are heavier than a Boxster S of the same vintage and a tenth or two slower in a straight line. The boot is small. The infotainment is a generation behind. None of this matters in the way the listings imply it matters.

The case for the V6 convertible

The market has, for reasons that probably involve YouTube and the V8 R's social-media legibility, decided that the F-Type you want is a coupe with the supercharged 5.0 and a manual gearbox. This is wrong on two counts. The manual was only ever fitted to the V6, the V6 R was never sold, and the V8 R is a car that — to its credit and its detriment — wants to be a Mustang most of the time. The V6 convertible is the F-Type that does the job the F-Type was designed for.

It is also, by some distance, the cheapest. Higher-mileage V6 S convertibles in good colours are now appearing on the major UK classifieds from around £20,000, with sub-60,000-mile cars sitting in the £24,000-28,000 band. A clean late car with a full Jaguar history, in the right spec, is a £30,000 proposition. For comparison, an equivalent-era Porsche 718 Boxster S starts at roughly £35,000 and goes up sharply from there.

What you are buying for that £20,000 is a 380-mile motorway cruise that doesn't tire you out, a soundtrack that is, even in the cooking V6, theatrically tuned, and a car that has - for now - no replacement on the horizon. Jaguar wound the F-Type down at the end of 2024 and confirmed there will not be another internal-combustion sports car from Coventry. The model is, as of this issue, finite.

What to look for, what to walk away from

The supercharged AJ126 V6 is, by Jaguar standards, an undramatic engine to own. The water pump is the well-known service item - replace it before 60,000 miles whether it's whining or not, because when it fails it takes the timing chain with it and you write a five-figure cheque. The supercharger snout bearing on early cars is a known wear item but is straightforward to address. Ignore any car that hasn't had a recent gearbox service; the ZF unit is robust but ATF-sensitive.

What to walk away from: cars with patchy or absent service history (the V6 will run for a long time on neglect, and then quite suddenly stop), any convertible whose roof mechanism feels notchy in operation, and any car whose seller cannot point to a recent diff oil change. The rear differential on the four-wheel-drive cars is a separate cost centre we will not discuss here. Buy a rear-wheel-drive V6 S convertible, in a colour you'd photograph, over Tan.

The case, in one paragraph

The Jaguar F-Type V6 convertible is the cheapest way, in 2026, to own a proper British sports car with a real soundtrack and a roof that actually goes down. The market has not noticed because the market is busy looking at the V8 R. Buy the cleanest example you can find. Service the water pump on the way home from collecting it. And spec, if you have any say in the matter, Salsa over Tan.

Buy.

Our take · Issue 015

The V6 convertible is the F-Type Jaguar should have been telling us about all along. From £20,000 it is, by a meaningful margin, the best-value modern British sports car on the market.

The model is now finite. The replacement is, by every public statement out of Coventry, electric and not coming soon. The car you want is a clean V6 S, in Salsa, over Tan - preferably parked, in the right light, somewhere that already feels like a postcard.
